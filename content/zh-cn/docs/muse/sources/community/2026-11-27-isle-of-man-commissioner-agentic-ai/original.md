Consumer facing agentic AI - A blog from the Information Commissioner

Published On:Friday, November 27, 2026


**Alexandra Delaney-Bhattachrya, Isle of Man Information Commissioner, discusses agentic AI and the release of Meta Muse.**

**What is agentic AI?**

Agentic AI refers to AI systems that combine the capabilities of generative AI with additional tools and the ability to interact with their environment to autonomously perform tasks, make decisions, and achieve defined objectives. These systems can plan, adapt, and automate increasingly complex, open-ended activities with varying levels of human oversight using contextual information and natural human language. 

Agentic AI is being developed for applications across research, coding, planning, transactions, commerce, healthcare, business and cyber security. It can also support advanced personal assistants that manage tasks and services on behalf of people. For example, an AI personal assistant could manage everyday activities such as arranging travel, comparing prices, booking appointments, making reservations, and managing household bills, potentially interacting with multiple services on a person’s behalf. 

**Why this matters now**

AI agents are already in use, but until recently they have rarely been aimed so directly at everyday consumers. Muse, a personal AI agent from Meta, launched in the UK on 24 November 2026 alongside partnerships with well-known brands including Sainsbury's, Marks & Spencer, easyJet and Trainline. Reports suggest Isle of Man residents may now be able to access it through the same app stores and services. 

Muse is designed to act on your behalf: reading and sending email, filling in forms, booking travel, making purchases and negotiating with others. For example, Muse could contact a company to pursue a refund or attempt to secure a better deal from a service provider. To do this, it asks for access to your accounts, messages and payment methods. It first launched in the United States in September 2026 and has since been made available in Canada, Singapore and Australia, for users aged 18 and over. It is a separate product from Meta AI, the assistant already built into WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook. 

Muse is one of the first AI agents of its kind to reach a wide public audience, and others are likely to follow.  

**What this means for your personal information**

For people to benefit from consumer AI products and services, they must have confidence that their personal information is handled lawfully and responsibly. This means having clarity about what information an AI agent can access, the decisions and actions it is authorised to make, and the ability to retain meaningful control over how it operates. People should be able to trust that organisations developing and providing these technologies have appropriate safeguards in place to protect their privacy and prevent harm. 

It is important to remember that agentic AI remains an emerging technology. Many of its capabilities and applications are still being tested and developed. Its increasing autonomy and access to personal information introduce important data protection, privacy, transparency, and cyber security risks. These risks potentially include unclear accountability across supply chains, excessive data access, unintended processing of sensitive information and increased automated decision-making. 

A conventional chatbot generally provides information or suggestions for a person to consider. An agentic AI system may go further and act on the information it generates. This means that mistakes can have consequences beyond an inaccurate response. For example, an agent could send information to the wrong recipient, make an unintended purchase, or change a booking without the user fully understanding what has happened. The more tasks an agent can perform autonomously, the more important it becomes to have appropriate safeguards, approval mechanisms, and ways to review or reverse its actions. 

Agentic AI also raises new questions about transparency and meaningful user control. People may give an agent permission to access information or perform certain tasks without fully understanding the extent of that permission, particularly where access is ongoing rather than limited to a single task. As agents become more capable and their activities more complex, it may become harder for people to understand what information is being accessed, how it is being used and what actions are being taken on their behalf. Organisations should ensure that permissions are clear, proportionate and easy to review or withdraw. 

**Things to think about with Muse**

[Meta says it has placed privacy at the centre of how it presents Muse](https://www.meta.com/help/superintelligence/1047255454427887/), including a dedicated secure cloud environment, approval steps before sensitive actions, and controls to disconnect services. We welcome these measures. However, early experience and independent analysis suggest some areas that people may wish to think about carefully. 

- **The amount of access involved**. An agent that can read your inbox, use your payment details and act in your name holds a great deal of personal information in one place. The more it is connected to, the greater the potential impact if something goes wrong.

- **Other people's information**. Muse may process information about your family, friends and colleagues, including people who have never used it or agreed to it, with reports suggesting that anyone who messages a Muse user may have their messages read by the agent.

- **Acting without you noticing**. There have been reported cases of Muse sharing personal details, such as a home address, in ways the user did not expect. Broad permissions such as "allow always" may remove the chance to check before something is shared.

- **Training and retention**. Reporting indicates that interactions may be used to train Meta's AI models unless you opt out. Information is held in Meta's cloud, and Meta has said stronger encryption options are planned rather than currently available.

- **Security**. Security researchers have already identified potential weaknesses in the software. As with any new technology, further issues may emerge.

- **Children and young people.** Muse is intended for adults, but its friendly design and promotion within apps widely used by young people may make it appealing to them.

**If you choose to use Muse or another AI agent**

The decision to use an AI agent is yours. If you do, you may find it helpful to: 

- Connect only the accounts you need for a particular task, and review those connections regularly;

- Start with the most limited approval settings, and think twice before granting standing permissions, particularly for sharing contact or location details;

- Check the settings on model training and decide whether you are comfortable with your conversations being used;

- Bear in mind that others in your contacts, messages and emails have not chosen to use the agent; and

- Use the activity history to check what the agent has done on your behalf, and find out how to view, edit, or delete what it remembers.

**For organisations**

If staff connect work email, calendars, or systems to a personal AI agent, your organisation's personal data, and that of your customers, may be processed outside your control. Organisations may wish to consider whether their acceptable use policies address personal AI agents, and remain responsible for meeting their obligations under the Applied GDPR.  

Organisations developing, deploying, or integrating agentic AI should adopt a privacy by design and default approach from the outset. This means considering the data protection implications of how an agent is designed, what information and systems it can access, what decisions it can make, and what actions it can take. Organisations should be able to demonstrate how they comply with their data protection obligations on an ongoing basis, including how they identify, assess and mitigate risks to individuals and the wider public. 

In November 2026 the ICO signed the Global Privacy Assembly (GPA) [resolution on privacy-protective agentic AI](https://globalprivacyassembly.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Resolution-on-Privacy-protective-Agentic-AI.pdf), which highlights the risks of ungoverned use of AI agents in organisations and encourages clear governance, impact assessments and meaningful human oversight. 

**Our approach** 

We are monitoring the development of Muse and similar AI agents and will be in contact with fellow regulators in the Crown Dependencies and internationally. We note that the UK Information Commission’s Office (ICO) engaged with Meta ahead of the UK launch, and that Meta has agreed to a voluntary monitoring and reporting scheme with the UK ICO. The UK ICO and the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) have also published joint advice for people using AI agents, which this advisory reflects.  

We expect organisations introducing agentic AI services to consider their data protection obligations before bringing products to market, rather than addressing privacy risks retrospectively. Early engagement with regulators can help organisations understand their responsibilities, identify potential risks and develop appropriate safeguards. Innovation and the protection of people's information should progress together. 

"AI agents like Muse may offer real convenience, but they ask for significant levels of access to our lives and to the lives of the people around us. I would encourage anyone considering them to take a moment to understand what they are sharing, and with whom." 

Dr Alexandra Delaney-Bhattacharya, Information Commissioner

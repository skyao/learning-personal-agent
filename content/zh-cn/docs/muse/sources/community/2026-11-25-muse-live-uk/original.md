## What Muse asks before it acts

Meta states the permission rule in its own post. Muse will ask for your permission before it sends an email or makes a purchase. In some cases it will ask for approval before other actions too, such as posting a photo or sharing something on your behalf, per Meta.

Meta also states the limits on what Muse sees. Muse will not ask for your passwords, bank account details or payment information, and you enter those yourself in the secure checkout, per Meta. Muse cannot see your passwords or credit card details, per the same post. Meta says it does not sell your data or share your conversations with advertisers, and that a security agent checks actions against your permissions, your task and your rules.

The Guardian, in Dan Milmo's report dated November 24, 2026 and read in full that day, states the same rule in shorter form: Muse cannot make a purchase or send an email without your permission, and it has no visibility into passwords or payment methods.

Hold both parts at once. The permission rule is printed by Meta and reported the same way by the Guardian. How often that rule interrupts real use is a different question, and the BBC trial answers it below.

## Checkout, on Meta's wording

When you are ready to buy, you can check out using Stripe Link or Shop Pay, per Meta. PayPal is not a launch option. Meta says it is working on adding PayPal as a checkout option in the coming months.

That distinction is worth keeping straight in a shopping week. A UK reader who plans to pay with PayPal through Muse does not have that route on the launch record. A reader with Stripe Link or Shop Pay does, subject to the approval step before checkout that Meta describes for partner websites.

## The BBC trial: helpful, awkward, and interrupted a lot

Zoe Kleinman, the BBC's Technology and AI editor, spent a week with Muse before the UK launch. Her article, dated November 24, 2026 and read in full that day, carries the verdict in its title: helpful but awkward.

What worked, in her account: Muse worked in the background on holiday options and reported back, it summarised incoming email and suggested replies, and it found train tickets. What did not work is in the same article, and it is specific. Muse could not book train tickets through the cheaper app she uses, and told her to book them herself. Amazon had blocked Muse from its marketplace, citing data security concerns, per the BBC, and her agent spent an hour saying the page was still loading while she waited for shelf results. She also reports replies that went too far, proactive prompts that arrived when she had not asked for anything, and exchanges that looped on pleasantries.

Field note

She connected personal email, calendar and phone messages, and did not allow access to BBC accounts, per the article. She gave her Muse a name, Linda, and a British female accent. None of those statements is a finding that Muse broke a rule in the UK. They are launch day positions from named regulators, printed here at that width.

Helpful but awkward: My week with Meta's Muse ahead of its UK launch

Zoe Kleinman, Technology & AI editor

[图片：路透社照片——男子走过纽约地铁里的 Meta Muse 广告]

I've just spent a week using Muse, Meta's AI agent that rolls out in the UK today.

Agents are systems that don't just respond to questions but can carry out tasks for you. With Muse, that could be anything from booking a table at a restaurant or making a travel plan, to sorting your emails and organising your diary.

And it's true that it has helped me run my life - but it also challenged my personal privacy boundaries and left me questioning whether the AI has really thought through how to make me comfortable sharing my most sensitive information with it.

How well Muse works really matters to Meta.

It has made a truly enormous bet on AI - of the $145bn (£111bn) it will spend this year, much of it will be on building out the tech.

For that to pay off we all need to start using it much more, and Meta hopes Muse will do for AI agents what ChatGPT did for chatbots.

But it's not just about what consumers think. Regulators are keeping a careful eye on these emerging technologies.

"Agentic AI has significant potential to benefit people and businesses", the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) said in a statement to the BBC.

But its chief executive, Paul Arnold, added it had been "clear with Meta about its responsibilities to safeguard people's personal information and the need to test the product thoroughly, given its role in accessing personal data on behalf of users."

## How does Muse work?

Muse bills itself as a personal AI agent available to anyone aged 18 and over.

"It doesn't just answer questions, it actually goes out and does work for you," said David Singleton, Meta's chief technology officer.

"So it helps people stay on top of things," he added. "[It] helps you take tasks and projects off your plate and just gives you that feeling of accomplishment."

What this means is you can set it up to do something for you, like find some specific holiday options for you, and then ask it to do the next step for you too.

It won't book the holiday without your permission - but it can then make the purchase, if you let it.

And the permission requests keep on coming. Every time Muse wants to do something, send something or buy something on your behalf it needs your approval.

It is reassuring to know that an AI agent isn't running amok in your name, even if it also gets a bit tedious.

Muse can't access many apps at the moment but the fundamental ones are the most personal.

I found myself wincing as I hooked it up to one of my personal email accounts, my personal calendar and my banking app.

It can't access WhatsApp except to set up a separate chat between it and the user, and while it found some potential restaurants for a night out with friends, it couldn't get me the tables.

It ended up telling me to book them myself.

Amazon has blocked it from its marketplace, citing data security concerns but my agent didn't seem to have a problem ordering a gift from the Marks & Spencer online store, subject to my approval of course.

A number of big UK firms are working with Meta though, including Marks & Spencer, Debenhams Group, Currys, Ticketmaster, Ocado and Deliveroo.

The ICO is urging consumers to be cautious with AI agents though: "consider the pros and cons," it said in a blog post to coincide with Muse's launch.

"Agents can make mistakes. Mistakes can be magnified if the agent is acting autonomously," it noted.

## Introducing Linda

Meta is keen to emphasise how its agents can be personalised: "No two Muses are alike," it says in its press release.

I called my agent Linda - after the robot in the BBC TV series Ann Droid - but it can take any form, including a human-like voice.

Linda is proactive. As soon as an email came in, it would summarise it for me and suggest a reply. Sometimes, those replies were helpful and prompt.

Sometimes, if I hadn't interacted with it for a while, generally because I was busy, it would pop up with a little "hello" - just checking in.

It also wrote some embarrassing replies to messages on my behalf: in response to a question about whether I wanted to come to a pub quiz, it replied "no" on my behalf before I had a chance to decide myself.

It seemed to want to have the last word quite a bit, occasionally getting stuck in a loop of "okay" and "got it" before eventually saying "bye".

Meta is relying on people choosing to trust it with even more of their personal data, despite a rather chequered reputation when it comes to safeguarding it.

I asked Linda - my new dedicated if occasionally clunky assistant - if it wanted to take people's jobs.

"Everything you do is stuff I would usually think about," I responded.

"That's fair and you're right," Linda replied, adding: "The difference, I hope, is that you're still in the driving seat and I'm just in the passenger seat helping you stay on track. If we keep the trust, the relationship will grow."

Which would have been more reassuring if the day before Muse's UK launch, Meta's head of AI Alexandr Wang hadn't issued a warning about how difficult it will be for humans to remain in control once AI is 1,000 times more intelligent than us.

And Linda is helping fuel that revolution, one calendar invite at a time.
